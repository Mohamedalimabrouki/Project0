See it

When you push a swing at just the right moments, small pushes add up and the swing goes higher and higher.

Everything that can wiggle, from a swing to a bridge, has its own favourite rhythm. Push it at that rhythm and a tiny force can make a huge movement. That is resonance.

Understand it

m ẍ + c ẋ + k x = F0 cos ωt

Natural frequency
ωn = √(k/m)
Damping ratio
ζ = c / (2√(km))
At resonance
amplitude = (F0/k) × 1/(2ζ)
Why it grows
the motion lags the push by 90°, so the push always works with the velocity: P = F·v ≥ 0
The model in the film
m = 2 kg, k = 79 N/m, fn = 1 Hz, ζ = 5 %, F0 = 0.5 N, so ±63 mm at resonance (10 × the 6.3 mm static stretch)

The two real cases

Checked against published sources

London Millennium Bridge

Opened
10 June 2000, closed 12 June 2000
Sideways sway
up to about 70 mm
Cause
walkers shifting their feet for balance pushed in step with the deck's motion, like negative damping
Fix
37 viscous dampers and about 60 tuned mass dampers
Reopened
22 February 2002

A popular myth says people marched in step. Research shows the balance behaviour alone is enough; falling into step came after the sway, not before it.

Taipei 101

Tower
508 m, first sway rhythm about 0.15 Hz (6.8 s)
Damper
660 t steel sphere, 5.5 m wide, 8 cables, 8 hydraulic dampers
Trick
tuned to the tower's rhythm, it swings about 90° behind, so its pull always opposes the tower's motion
Effect
up to about 40 % less sway (operator figure)
Largest swing
1 m, Typhoon Soudelor, 8 August 2015

It is designed for comfort in wind, not as earthquake protection.
